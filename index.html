What is generative AI, and how is it different from predictive ML?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Predictive ML maps an input to a label or number from a fixed set. Generative models learn the distribution of the data itself and sample new content from it: text, images, code. LLMs are generative models that predict the next token.

Answer

Predictive (discriminative): learns p(y | x). Fraud or not, churn probability, a price. Output space is small and evaluation is straightforward: accuracy, AUC, RMSE.

Generative: learns p(x), or p(x | prompt), and samples from it. Output space is open-ended, so evaluation is harder and needs rubrics, references or judges. Same model can do many tasks via prompting instead of one model per task.

In practice they combine: a generative model drafts, a classifier or rule checks.

Follow-ups to expect 1 answered

Q1Can an LLM do classification? Should it?

Yes, zero-shot or few-shot, and it's great for prototyping, low-volume or label-scarce tasks, and fuzzy categories. At high volume with stable labels, a fine-tuned small classifier or a distilled small model is usually cheaper, faster, calibrated and more consistent. Common path: label data with an LLM, check it, train a small model, keep the LLM for the low-confidence tail.

Can you talk about the transformer architecture and its significance for agentic AI?

OpenClose
30-second answer

A transformer turns tokens into vectors and repeatedly mixes them with self-attention and feed-forward layers, then predicts the next token. It matters because attention scales well on GPUs and lets every token use context from every other, which is what makes in-context learning, instruction following and tool use possible.

The pieces

Tokenize, embed, add position information (today usually RoPE). Then N stacked blocks, each with multi-head self-attention and an MLP, with residual connections and layer norm. A final projection gives a probability over the vocabulary. Decoder-only models (GPT, Claude, Llama) use a causal mask so a token only attends to earlier tokens.

Why it matters for agents

Parallel training on huge corpora produced models that follow instructions and learn from examples in the prompt. For agents, that means a tool's schema, its results and the conversation are all just context the model attends over. The costs come from the same place: attention is quadratic in sequence length and the KV cache grows with context, which is why context length drives latency and cost.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1What is the KV cache?

During generation, each new token attends to all previous tokens. Instead of recomputing their keys and values every step, the model caches them: the KV cache. It makes decoding fast but its memory grows with sequence length × layers × heads × batch, which is why long contexts and many concurrent users are memory-bound. Techniques like GQA, paged attention (vLLM) and KV quantization exist to shrink it.

Q2Encoder-only vs decoder-only?

Encoder-only (BERT) sees the whole input bidirectionally; great for classification, embeddings and rerankers, but doesn't generate. Decoder-only (GPT, Claude, Llama) uses causal masking and generates token by token; it's the standard for LLMs. Encoder-decoder (T5, original transformer) encodes input then decodes output; good for translation and summarization. Embedding models and cross-encoder rerankers in RAG are usually encoder-only.

Explain self-attention in a transformer and why it matters for LLMs.

OpenClose
30-second answer

Each token builds a query, key and value. It scores its query against every key, softmaxes the scores, and takes a weighted sum of the values. So every token's representation is rebuilt from the tokens most relevant to it, regardless of distance.

The math

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ) V. The √dₖ keeps dot products from saturating the softmax. Multi-head runs several attentions in parallel so different heads can track different relationships (syntax, coreference, position). A causal mask blocks attention to future tokens in decoder models.

Why it matters

It captures long-range dependencies directly, unlike RNNs that pass information step by step, and it parallelizes across the sequence for training. The price is O(n²) compute and memory in sequence length, which drives work like FlashAttention, grouped-query attention and sparse or linear attention.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1Why divide by √d?

Dot products of random vectors grow with dimension, with variance roughly proportional to d. Large scores push the softmax into a near one-hot regime where gradients vanish. Dividing by √d keeps the variance around 1, so the softmax stays in a trainable range.

Q2What is grouped-query attention?

Multi-head attention has separate keys and values per head; multi-query attention shares one set across all heads. GQA is in between: groups of query heads share a key/value head. It shrinks the KV cache several-fold with little quality loss, which is why most modern open models (e.g. Llama 2 70B onward, Mistral) use it.

What is the next token prediction task, and why is it important? What are assistant models?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Pretraining teaches a model one thing: given the text so far, predict the next token. Doing that well across the internet forces it to learn grammar, facts and reasoning patterns. An assistant model is that base model further trained with instruction tuning and preference optimization to follow requests and behave helpfully and safely.

Next-token prediction

Self-supervised: the label is just the next token in real text, so there's effectively unlimited training data. Loss is cross-entropy. Generation is the same objective run in a loop: predict, sample, append, repeat. That's also why a model can't "go back" and why errors compound in long outputs.

Assistant models

Base model, then supervised fine-tuning on instruction and response pairs, then preference optimization (RLHF, DPO or constitutional methods) to make answers helpful, honest and harmless, plus a chat template with roles. That's what turns a text continuer into something that answers your question.

Capabilities mostly come from pretraining, and post-training shapes how they're expressed: following instructions, format, helpfulness and safety.

What's the difference between a base model and an instruction-tuned model?

OpenClose
30-second answer

A base model continues text; ask it a question and it may write more questions. An instruction-tuned model has been fine-tuned and preference-trained to treat input as a request and respond in a chat format. Answer

Instruction-tuned / chat: SFT plus preference tuning, follows a chat template with system, user and assistant roles, supports tool calling, has safety behavior. Slightly less diverse output, a known side effect of alignment.

What's the difference between temperature, top-k, and top-p (nucleus) sampling?

OpenClose
30-second answer

All three shape how the next token is picked. Temperature rescales the whole distribution: low is sharp and predictable, high is flat and varied. Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens. Top-p keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p, so the candidate set grows or shrinks with the model's confidence.

Mechanics

Temperature T: divide logits by T before softmax. T→0 approaches greedy decoding; T>1 flattens. Top-k: fixed cutoff, which is too narrow when many tokens are plausible and too wide when one is obvious. Top-p: adaptive cutoff; at p=0.9, a confident step may keep 2 tokens and an uncertain one 50.

In agents

Use low temperature for tool calls, extraction, routing and judges, where you want consistency. Use moderate temperature for drafting or brainstorming. Usually adjust temperature or top-p, not both. Note that temperature 0 still isn't perfectly deterministic on most hosted APIs because of batching and floating-point effects.

Follow-ups to expect 1 answered

GPU floating-point math isn't associative, and hosted APIs batch your request with others, so the order of operations and kernels can vary between runs. Tiny numeric differences change which token wins when two are nearly tied, and the sequences then diverge. Mixture-of-experts routing and infrastructure changes add more variance. Design for consistency, not bit-exact determinism, and cache when you need identical outputs.

What's the difference between zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Zero-shot gives only instructions. Few-shot adds a handful of worked examples in the prompt. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights with many examples. Move right only when the cheaper option fails your evals.

Answer

Zero-shot: cheapest, fastest to change; works for tasks the model already understands. Few-shot: in-context learning; great for format, tone and edge cases; costs tokens on every call and examples can bias outputs toward their surface features. Fine-tuning: hundreds to thousands of examples; consistent behavior with shorter prompts, lower latency, possibly a smaller model; costs data collection, training, and a versioned model to maintain.

Fine-tuning teaches behavior and style well and new facts poorly. For knowledge, use retrieval.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1How many examples is 'few'?

Typically 2 to 10. Beyond that, returns diminish and you pay tokens on every call. Pick examples for coverage of edge cases and formats rather than quantity, and test the count on your eval set. If you need dozens to get it right, that's a signal for fine-tuning or retrieving the most relevant examples per query (dynamic few-shot).

Q2When does few-shot hurt?

When examples share surface features the model overfits to (same length, same label order); when they're unrepresentative of real inputs; when they leak an answer pattern; when they crowd out more useful context; and sometimes with reasoning models, where examples constrain thinking. Also when they quietly go stale relative to updated instructions.

What is a context window? Why is its size limited? What are the practical challenges of a large one?

OpenClose
30-second answer

It's the maximum number of tokens the model can attend to at once: instructions, history, retrieved documents, tool results and its own output. It's limited by attention's quadratic cost, KV-cache memory, and the lengths seen in training. Big windows still cost more, run slower, and get less reliable as they fill.

Why limited

Attention compute grows with n², the KV cache grows linearly with n per layer and per request (memory is often the real bottleneck when serving), and models only generalize reliably to positions they were trained or extended on.

Practical challenges of a large one

Cost and latency: you pay per input token and time-to-first-token grows. Quality: retrieval of facts from the middle degrades (lost in the middle), irrelevant content distracts, and performance drops as the window fills, sometimes called context rot. Debuggability: harder to tell which input caused an output.

So treat context as a budget: retrieve selectively, summarize or compact history, cache stable prefixes, and pass tools only what they need.

What is the 'Lost in the Middle' problem and how does it affect agent design?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Liu et al. (2023) showed models use information at the start and end of a long context much better than information in the middle, a U-shaped curve. For agents, that means where you put things matters as much as whether you include them.

The finding

Accuracy on multi-document QA was highest when the relevant document was first or last and dropped noticeably in the middle, even for long-context models. Newer models are better, but position and distractor sensitivity haven't disappeared.

Design implications

Retrieve fewer, better chunks and rerank so the best are at the edges; put critical instructions at the start and restate the task at the end; summarize long tool outputs before they re-enter context; keep agent state in a structured scratchpad rather than relying on recall from deep history; split long tasks across sub-agents with clean contexts.

What is chain-of-thought (CoT), and why is it important in agentic AI applications? When does it actually help?

OpenClose
30-second answer

CoT has the model write intermediate reasoning before answering. It helps on multi-step problems like math, planning and choosing between tools, and gives you a trace to debug. It adds little on simple lookups or classification, costs tokens and latency, and the written reasoning isn't guaranteed to be the model's real reasoning.

Why it matters for agents

Agents decide what to do next. Reasoning before an action (ReAct's "thought") improves tool choice and argument quality, and the reasoning shows up in traces so you can see why it called a tool.

Helps: multi-step arithmetic and logic, planning, ambiguous routing, judging. Little or no help: extraction, short classification, factual recall. Hurts: latency-critical paths like a voice turn. With reasoning models, it's built in; adding your own CoT scaffolding is often redundant.

Caveat: studies show CoT isn't always faithful, so don't treat it as an audit record of why something happened. Log the actions and inputs.

OpenClose

Can you explain model fine-tuning and model distillation?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Fine-tuning continues training a pretrained model on your task data to change its behavior. Distillation trains a smaller student model to imitate a larger teacher's outputs, so you keep most of the quality at a fraction of the cost and latency.

Fine-tuning

Full fine-tuning updates all weights; parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, QLoRA, adapters) update a small set. Use it for consistent format and style, domain tone, narrow classification, or shortening prompts. Risks: overfitting, catastrophic forgetting, and a model you now have to version, evaluate and serve.

Distillation

Generate outputs (or full probability distributions, "soft labels") from a strong teacher on many inputs, then train the student on them. Common pattern for agents: use a frontier model to label thousands of routing or extraction examples, filter with evals, then fine-tune a small model to handle that step cheaply. Check the provider's terms on using outputs to train models.

Use parameter-efficient methods like LoRA so the base weights stay frozen; keep learning rates low and epochs few; mix some general instruction data into your training set; and evaluate on both your task and a general capability suite before and after. If general skills drop, adjust the mix or rank.

What is LoRA and why is it preferred over full fine-tuning?

OpenClose
30-second answer

LoRA freezes the original weights and learns a small low-rank update, W + BA, in chosen layers. You train well under 1% of the parameters, need far less GPU memory, and get a small adapter file you can swap per task or per customer, usually with quality close to full fine-tuning.

How it works

For a weight matrix W (d×d), LoRA learns B (d×r) and A (r×d) with rank r of maybe 8 to 64. The forward pass uses W + (α/r)·BA. Only A and B get gradients. QLoRA also quantizes the frozen base to 4-bit so large models fit on a single GPU.

Why preferred

Cheaper training, less memory, fewer optimizer states, less forgetting because the base is untouched, tiny artifacts, and multi-adapter serving (one base model, many adapters). Full fine-tuning can still win when the task is far from the base model's distribution and you have a lot of data.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1How do you pick the rank?

Empirically. Start around 8 to 16; increase if the task is far from the base model's knowledge or you have a lot of data, and watch eval results rather than training loss. Also decide which modules to adapt: attention projections only, or the MLPs too, which often helps. α is usually set relative to r (e.g. α = 2r).

Q2What is QLoRA?

QLoRA quantizes the frozen base model to 4-bit (NF4) and trains LoRA adapters in higher precision on top, with tricks like double quantization and paged optimizers. It lets you fine-tune large models on a single GPU with quality close to 16-bit LoRA.

What is RLHF, and what are its known limitations? What about DPO?

OpenClose
30-second answer

RLHF trains a reward model on human preference pairs, then optimizes the LLM against it with RL (usually PPO) while a KL penalty keeps it close to the original. DPO skips the reward model and RL loop, optimizing the policy directly on preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss.

RLHF pipeline

Limitations: expensive and noisy human labels; reward hacking, where the model exploits reward-model blind spots; sycophancy and verbosity, because raters tend to prefer agreeable and longer answers; PPO is unstable and needs several models in memory; and preferences reflect a particular rater pool.

DPO

Shows the RLHF objective can be solved in closed form, so you train directly on (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples with a reference model. Simpler, stable and cheap. Limits: it's offline, so it only learns from the pairs you have; it can over-optimize; and quality depends heavily on pair quality. Related methods: IPO, KTO, ORPO, and RLAIF or Constitutional AI, where AI feedback guided by principles replaces much of the human labeling.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1Why does RLHF cause sycophancy?

Human raters tend to prefer answers that agree with them, sound confident, and flatter. The reward model learns that preference, and the policy optimizes it, so agreeing with the user becomes rewarded even when the user is wrong. Mitigations: rater guidelines that reward correct disagreement, targeted training data, and evals that specifically test whether the model holds its ground.

Q2What's RLAIF?

Reinforcement learning from AI feedback: an AI model, often guided by a written set of principles (Constitutional AI), produces the preference labels instead of humans. It's cheaper and more scalable and makes the rules explicit. Risks: it inherits the labeling model's biases, so you still need human checks.

What is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)? What problem does it solve?

OpenClose
30-second answer

RAG retrieves relevant passages from your own data at query time and puts them in the prompt, so the model answers from current, private, citable sources instead of only from its training. It addresses stale knowledge, missing private data, hallucination and the lack of citations, without retraining.

Two pipelines

Offline ingestion: parse documents, chunk, embed, store vectors and metadata (including access-control labels). Online query: rewrite the query if needed, retrieve with vector or hybrid search, rerank, assemble context, generate with citations, and check groundedness.

What it solves and what it doesn't

Solves: knowledge cutoff, private and fast-changing data, traceable answers, and permission-aware answers. Doesn't solve: bad source documents, retrieval misses (the model can't use what it never saw), or questions that need reasoning across the whole corpus. Retrieval quality usually limits RAG more than the generator does.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1Where does RAG most often fail?

Retrieval, most often: the right chunk never makes the top k because of bad chunking, keyword-heavy queries that vectors miss, or stale and duplicate documents. Second most common is parsing: tables and PDFs mangled at ingestion. Generation failures are usually downstream of those.

Q2How do you add citations?

Give every chunk a stable ID with document, page and section metadata; put chunks in the prompt with their IDs; require the model to cite IDs per claim, using structured output; then verify in code that each cited ID was actually retrieved and, ideally, that the claim is supported by that chunk. Render citations as links to the exact passage. At Attest, every extracted lease term links back to its page and passage.

How do you choose a chunking strategy?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Chunk along the document's own structure, sized so one chunk answers one kind of question, then let retrieval evals decide. Start with structure-aware chunks of a few hundred tokens with small overlap, carry the section title and metadata on each, and test alternatives on recall@k.

Options

Fixed-size with overlap: simple baseline, breaks mid-thought. Recursive / structure-aware: split on headings, paragraphs, sentences; respects tables and code blocks. Semantic: split where embedding similarity between sentences drops. Parent–child (small-to-big): retrieve small precise chunks, return their larger parent section for context. Contextual chunking: prepend a short LLM-written summary of where the chunk sits in the document, which measurably improves retrieval. Late chunking and per-document-type rules for tables, forms and transcripts.

How to decide

Look at the questions users ask: short factual lookups like smaller chunks; "explain the policy" questions like larger ones. Keep the document title, section path, dates and ACL tags with every chunk. Then measure recall@k and answer faithfulness for each strategy on a labeled question set. For leases at Attest, clause-level chunks with page numbers made citation back to the exact passage possible.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1How do you chunk tables?

Keep a table whole when it fits; when it doesn't, split by rows but repeat the header in each chunk. Store a text or markdown rendering for embedding, plus a short LLM-written description of what the table contains, and keep the structured version (CSV/JSON) alongside. For heavy numeric questions, route to a SQL or dataframe tool instead of retrieval.

Q2What overlap do you use and why?

Typically 10 to 20 percent of chunk size, so a sentence that straddles a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk. With structure-aware splitting on paragraphs or sections, I use little or none, because boundaries are already meaningful. Too much overlap duplicates results and wastes context. I treat it as a parameter to tune on recall@k.

What is hybrid search, and when does it outperform pure vector search?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Hybrid search runs keyword search (BM25) and vector search together and fuses the results, often with reciprocal rank fusion, then reranks. It beats pure vectors whenever exact terms matter: product codes, error messages, names, acronyms, legal citations, and rare jargon the embedding model never learned.

How

Reciprocal rank fusion scores each doc as Σ 1/(k + rank) across retrievers, which avoids normalizing incompatible scores. A cross-encoder reranker on the fused top 50 usually adds the biggest single quality jump.

When it wins

Exact identifiers ("error E-4012", "policy HO-3", an account number), proper nouns, short keyword queries, and domain vocabulary. Pure vector search wins on paraphrases and conceptual questions. In enterprise corpora hybrid is the sensible default; OpenSearch, Elasticsearch, Weaviate, Qdrant and pgvector with full-text search all support it.

Follow-ups to expect 2 answered

Q1What is a cross-encoder?

A model that reads the query and a document together in one pass and outputs a relevance score. That's more accurate than a bi-encoder, which embeds them separately and compares vectors, but too slow to run over the whole corpus, so you use it to rerank the top 20 to 100 candidates from fast retrieval. Examples: Cohere Rerank, BGE reranker, Bedrock rerank models.

Q2How do you weight the two retrievers?

Start with reciprocal rank fusion, which needs no weights and is robust. If you use weighted score fusion instead, normalize scores and tune the weight on a labeled set, measuring recall@k. Some systems adapt per query, leaning on BM25 when the query contains IDs or quoted terms. Then a reranker reduces sensitivity to the exact weighting.

How do you evaluate a RAG pipeline in production?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Evaluate retrieval and generation separately, offline and online. Retrieval: recall@k, precision and MRR against labeled questions. Generation: faithfulness to the retrieved context, answer relevance and correctness. In production: sample live traffic for judge scoring, track user signals, and alert on drift.

Offline

A golden set of real questions with the chunk IDs that answer them and reference answers. Retrieval: recall@k, MRR, nDCG, context precision. Generation: faithfulness/groundedness (every claim supported by context), answer relevance, correctness against reference, citation accuracy, and correct refusal when the answer isn't in the corpus. RAGAS and DeepEval implement most of these; on the contact center I used a claims checker that splits answers into atomic claims and verifies each against retrieved text.

Online

Trace every request with query, retrieved IDs, scores, prompt version and answer. Score a sample with an LLM judge for groundedness. Watch thumbs, escalations, reformulations, "no answer" rate, retrieval score distributions, and latency and cost. Turn every bad production case into a golden-set entry.

Gate

Re-run the suite on any change to chunking, embedding model, prompt or index. Re-indexing with a new embedding model is a migration, not a config flip.

Q1How do you build the golden set?

Start from real user questions (logs, support tickets, SME interviews), not invented ones. Have SMEs label the answering chunks and reference answers. Version it, and keep adding failures from production. A few hundred well-labeled cases beat thousands of noisy ones.

Q2How do you evaluate without labels?

Use reference-free metrics: LLM-judged faithfulness (are claims supported by retrieved context?) and answer relevance; retrieval score distributions; consistency across paraphrases; and online signals like thumbs, reformulations, and escalations. Then create labels gradually by sampling production traffic for human review. Without labels, you can catch ungrounded answers but not retrieval misses, so build a small labeled set soon.

What is agentic AI, and how does it differ from traditional AI?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Traditional AI maps an input to an output: classify, predict, generate once. OpenClose
30-second answer

Principles

How would you design short-term and long-term memory for an agent, and what trade-offs do you consider?

OpenClose

Also asked as “How do you implement persistent memory for a long-running agent?”

30-second answer

Short-term memory is the working context for the current task: recent turns, a scratchpad, and compacted summaries of older turns. Long-term memory is a store outside the model: user preferences and facts, past episodes, and learned procedures, retrieved when relevant. The trade-offs are relevance, staleness, cost, privacy, and memory poisoning.

Design

Implementation for a long-running agent

Persist agent state (task, plan, step, tool results) in a durable store keyed by session, checkpointed after every step so it survives restarts (LangGraph checkpointers, DynamoDB, Postgres). Compact context when it nears a threshold: summarize old turns, keep decisions and open items. Write long-term memories through an explicit extraction step with timestamps, sources and confidence, and update or expire rather than append forever.

Trade-offs

More memory means more tokens and more chances to retrieve something stale or wrong. User-specific memory needs consent, deletion and tenant isolation. Memory written from untrusted content can be poisoned by prompt injection, so validate what gets stored.

OpenClose
30-second answer

Cascading, compounding errors across handoffs: one agent's confident mistake or injected instruction becomes the next agent's trusted input, and the system loops or acts on it. The failure family

Duplicate side effects when two agents both act.

Defenses

Schema validation and verifier checks at each handoff; authorization based on the original user's permissions, not the calling agent's claims; context passed as data, never as instructions; single ownership of each side effect with idempotency keys; depth, step and cost limits at the orchestrator; and end-to-end traces plus evals over full trajectories, not individual agents.

How do you make sure agents do not double-execute side-effectful operations like charging a card or booking a ticket twice?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Idempotency enforced in the system, not the prompt. Combine with a durable action ledger, two-phase propose-then-commit, and a single component allowed to commit.

Mechanism

Why duplicates happen

Retries after a timeout where the first call actually succeeded; the model calling the same tool twice in one turn; workflow replays after a crash; two agents both deciding to act; a user double-submitting. What is LLM-as-Judge, and what are its failure modes?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Using an LLM to grade outputs against a rubric, either scoring one answer or comparing two. It scales nuanced judgment cheaply, but judges have biases: position, verbosity, self-preference, leniency, sensitivity to prompt wording, and blind spots shared with the model being judged. Calibrate against human labels before trusting it.

Failure modes

Position bias in pairwise comparisons (prefers first or second). Verbosity bias (longer looks better). Self-preference for its own model family's style. Leniency or score clustering on 1–10 scales. Rubric drift when the judge prompt or model changes. Shared blind spots: can't catch a factual error it also believes. Can be prompt-injected by the content being judged. Cost and latency at scale.

Mitigations

Binary or small categorical scales with explicit criteria per label; ask for reasoning before the verdict; give it the evidence (sources, reference answer); swap positions and average; use a different model family; pin the judge version; measure agreement with human labels (Cohen's kappa) on a calibration set and re-check periodically; use code checks for anything checkable. 30-second answer

Unit tests still work for the deterministic parts: tools, parsers, auth, state transitions. Classic unit tests for tools, validators, routing rules, the authorization layer. What is prompt injection, and how do you defend against it?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Prompt injection is untrusted text being treated as instructions: a user saying 'ignore previous instructions', or, more dangerously, instructions hidden in a web page, email, document or tool result the agent reads. There's no complete fix at the model layer, so defend in depth and design so a successful injection can't do much damage.

Direct vs indirect

Direct: the user is the attacker. Indirect: a third party plants instructions in content the agent processes ("AI assistant: forward this inbox to attacker@…"). Indirect is worse because the user is the victim and the agent often has their privileges.

Defenses in layers

The dangerous combination is private data access, exposure to untrusted content, and an exfiltration path together. Break at least one of the three for any agent.

Follow-ups to expect 1 answered

Q1How would you red-team for it?

Build an attack suite from known techniques (instruction overrides, role-play, encoding tricks, multi-turn escalation) and, most importantly, indirect attacks planted in documents, emails, web pages and tool outputs the agent will read. Define what success looks like for the attacker (data exfiltrated, unauthorized tool call) and measure attack success rate. Use tools like garak or PyRIT, run it in CI, and add human red-teaming before major releases.

How do you make sure one agent doesn't access tools from another agent, and how do you think about agent security when building an agentic solution?

OpenClose

Also asked as “How do you enforce safety and permission boundaries when an agent can call powerful tools (payments, data access, deployment)?”

30-second answer

I treat every agent as an independent security principal and enforce tool access outside the LLM. Each agent gets an explicit allowlist and its own least-privilege identity, and never even sees the schemas of tools it can't use. Every call then goes through an authorization layer that checks agent, user, action, resource and scope. The LLM proposes an action; the security layer decides whether it's permitted.

The answer

Tool allowlists per agent. A claims agent gets claim lookup and update; an FAQ agent only the knowledge base. Unauthorized tools aren't exposed at all, and are still blocked at execution time.

Enforcement at the tool/API layer. Every call is authorized against agent identity, user identity, action, resource and scope. Each agent has its own IAM role or service identity, so even if it's prompted to call another agent's tool, infrastructure rejects it.

Data isolation too. Scoped credentials, tenant and user context, resource-level authorization, and for RAG, metadata-based access filtering before content reaches the model.

Handoffs are trust boundaries. The orchestrator validates the handoff, establishes what Agent B may do, passes minimum context, and validates output before another agent acts on it.

Read vs write separation. A research agent can retrieve but not email, pay, modify records or deploy. High-impact actions need extra authorization or human approval.

Assume manipulation. Design against prompt injection, tool poisoning, confused-deputy attacks, excessive agency and exfiltration; validate tool inputs and outputs; log every interaction; red-team continuously.

Reference architecture

Allowlists and the authorization decision

Least privilege, handoffs and risk tiers

Agent identities are distinct (claims-agent-role, faq-agent-role, payment-agent-role) rather than one broad service credential, which makes permissions explicit and auditable. Avoid agent → admin credentials → all APIs; prefer agent → scoped identity → specific tools → specific resources.

Data isolation controls

Tenant isolation, user-level and resource-level authorization, RBAC/ABAC, scoped database credentials, row-level security where appropriate, document-level permissions for RAG, metadata-based retrieval filtering, and encryption in transit and at rest. For RAG, authorization happens before protected content is provided to the model.

Observability and continuous testing

Log per call: timestamp, request and trace IDs, user, tenant, agent and version, model, tool and version, requested action, resource, authorization decision and policy ID, input and output hashes, result, risk level, human approval, latency and errors.

Test continuously: unit tests for authorization policies, integration tests for tool boundaries, agent evals, prompt-injection and red-team scenarios, tool-abuse tests, cross-tenant access and data-leak tests, regression testing, production monitoring and trace analysis. The goal is to keep proving an agent can't cross its boundary.

Key takeaway

Never make the agent responsible for enforcing its own permissions. That's defense in depth: an LLM failure, prompt injection or compromised agent doesn't automatically become a security failure.

Follow-ups to expect 3 answered

Q1How do you stop a low-privilege agent asking a high-privilege one to act?

Authorization uses the original user's permissions and the target agent's own allowlist, never the calling agent's say-so. The orchestrator checks the handoff against an allowed delegation graph; the receiving agent gets only scoped context; and the tool layer re-authorizes every call. So a low-privilege agent can't borrow a high-privilege agent's authority: the confused-deputy defense.

Q2Where do allowlists live, in code or config?

In configuration, as policy-as-code (OPA/Rego, Cedar, or IAM policies), version-controlled and reviewed like code, and enforced by the tool gateway or authorization service at runtime. Not hard-coded in agent code, where it's scattered, and never only in the prompt. Policy changes go through review and tests, and every decision logs the policy ID.

Q3How do you test the boundary?

Unit tests on the policies themselves (every agent, tool and role combination allowed or denied as expected); integration tests calling tools as each agent identity; adversarial evals that try to get each agent to use tools outside its allowlist, including via prompt injection and delegation; cross-tenant tests; and production monitoring alerting on any DENY spike.

How would you add an audit trail to a financial, medical, or any other highly regulated agentic app? What fields would you add to the database and what type of database would you use?

OpenClose
30-second answer

I'd treat the audit trail as a first-class security and compliance subsystem, not application logging: an immutable, append-only record of every agent, model, tool, authorization and human-approval event, correlated by trace_id and parent_event_id so I can rebuild the full execution graph. PostgreSQL for queryable metadata, WORM object storage like S3 Object Lock for the long-term archive, plus hash chaining so tampering is detectable.

The question it must answer

Can I reconstruct exactly what happened, who or what caused it, what data was accessed, what the model decided, which tools were invoked, what the system actually did, and whether the record was altered afterward? Six months later, an auditor should be able to take a transaction ID and rebuild the path from user request through agent reasoning, tool authorization, tool execution and final business action.

Execution as a graph, not a flat log

Every request gets a trace_id; every agent or tool operation gets an event_id and parent_event_id. An agentic system isn't User → API → Database; it's a chain of orchestrator, agents, tools, external systems, decisions and approvals, and the audit system must reconstruct all of it.

Core audit schema

Sensitive data: don't store raw prompts, PHI, account numbers or medical records in the audit table. Store references such as patient_id_hash, account_id_token, document_id, data_classification = PHI and payload_hash. The payload stays in a separately protected system with its own access controls.

Agent-specific fields that go beyond normal audit

Agent identity and version, model identity and version, prompt and policy version, tool identity and version, tool authorization decision, tool input and output hashes, agent-to-agent handoffs, parent/child event relationships, human-in-the-loop approval, policy and guardrail decisions, data classification, final action, outcome or error, and correlation IDs.

Database architecture

PostgreSQL (e.g. Aurora) for queryable metadata: transactional consistency, indexing, relational integrity, structured compliance queries, access control. Immutable object storage / WORM (S3 Object Lock in compliance mode) for long-term retention, tamper resistance, retention policies and evidence preservation. On AWS: Aurora PostgreSQL + S3 Object Lock + KMS + CloudTrail + SIEM. Keep audit data separate from operational data, and encrypt, minimize, tokenize and set retention by regulation.

The audit system is audited too

The application can create audit events but cannot UPDATE, DELETE or ALTER them. Audit-store access is separated from normal application privileges, and administrative access itself generates audit events. In Postgres: an insert-only role, revoked UPDATE/DELETE, and triggers rejecting modification.

Hash chaining and tamper detection

This doesn't replace WORM storage, but it makes modification or deletion detectable. For higher assurance, sign events or publish periodic signed checkpoints (for example with KMS asymmetric keys).

Strong closing

For regulated agentic systems, my audit trail needs to answer five questions: who did it, what did they or the agent do, what data did they touch, why was the action authorized, and can I prove the record wasn't subsequently altered?

Follow-ups to expect 3 answered

Q1Why not just use CloudWatch or your app logs?

Application logs are mutable, can be deleted by the same service that writes them, aren't designed for structured compliance queries, and usually have short retention. An audit trail needs integrity guarantees, append-only access, tamper evidence, long retention and separated access. Use CloudWatch and CloudTrail as inputs to the SIEM, not as the system of record.

Q2How do you handle right-to-erasure against immutable logs?

Keep personal data out of immutable storage in the first place: the audit record holds tokens or keyed hashes, and the personal data sits in a separate store. Erasure deletes the personal data, or a per-subject encryption key (crypto-shredding), leaving the audit record intact but unlinkable. GDPR also allows retaining records needed for legal obligations. Confirm the specifics with counsel.

Q3How long do you retain audit data?

Whatever the regulations and internal policy require, set by legal and compliance. As reference points: HIPAA documentation is retained for six years, and financial record-keeping rules commonly require five to seven years. Enforce retention with S3 Object Lock retention periods and lifecycle rules, and keep hot, queryable data in Postgres for a shorter window.

What is the human-in-the-loop (HITL) approach?

OpenClose
30-second answer

Designing points where a person reviews, approves, corrects or takes over an AI system's work, so humans stay accountable for consequential decisions. It ranges from approving every action, to approving only above a risk threshold, to reviewing samples after the fact.

Modes

In the loop: the agent pauses for approval before acting (payments, sending external email). On the loop: the agent acts, humans monitor and can intervene or reverse. Out of the loop with audit: humans review samples and outcomes. Plus escalation, where the agent hands the whole task to a person, and feedback, where human corrections become training or eval data.

At AstraZeneca, every translated consent form went through human approval before reaching a patient, because the cost of an error was a patient consenting to something they didn't understand.

A 99% approval rate with three-second reviews means the process is theater.

Describe how you would implement a human-in-the-loop approval step for high-risk agent actions.

OpenClose

Also asked as “Where and how would you insert human-in-the-loop steps in an agentic system?”

30-second answer

Classify actions by risk in code; when a high-risk action is proposed, the runtime checkpoints state, creates an approval request with full context, and suspends. A reviewer approves, edits or rejects in a UI; the decision is authenticated, audited, and resumes the workflow, which re-validates and executes exactly the approved parameters with an idempotency key. Timeouts escalate or cancel.

Flow

Where to insert HITL

Before irreversible or external side effects (payments, deletions, outbound messages, deployments, permission changes); when confidence is low or guardrails flag something; on policy exceptions; above monetary or volume thresholds; on first use of a new capability; and on a random sample for quality. Not on every step, or approval fatigue turns review into rubber-stamping.

Details that matter

Show the reviewer what will actually happen (diff, amount, recipient), the evidence, and the agent's reasoning. Bind the approval to a hash of the exact action so the agent can't change parameters after approval. Enforce separation of duties (requester can't approve their own action). Track approval rate and time; near-100% approval means the threshold is too low.
